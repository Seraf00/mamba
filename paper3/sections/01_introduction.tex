\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two-dimensional echocardiography is the first-line modality for assessing
cardiac function. Delineation of the left-ventricular (LV) endocardium,
myocardium and left atrium underpins every downstream measurement, ejection
fraction (EF) chief among them, and automating it has been an active target
since the release of the CAMUS benchmark~\cite{leclerc2019camus}. Progress has
been rapid: encoder--decoder networks~\cite{ronneberger2015unet,isensee2021nnunet}
and transformer hybrids~\cite{chen2021transunet,cao2022swinunet} now routinely
report mean Dice above $0.90$.

That number deserves scrutiny. The authors of CAMUS report an average
inter-observer Dice of $0.899$ on this data~\cite{leclerc2019camus}: two
cardiologists tracing the same frames agree with one another to about that
level. A model scoring $0.92$ is therefore not $0.02$ away from being correct;
it is already past the point where the reference itself is stable, and further
gains in overlap increasingly measure conformity to one annotator's habits
rather than anatomical accuracy.

This paper asks what still moves accuracy in that regime, and answers it mostly
in the negative. We use real-time instance segmentation as the test bed, for two
reasons. First, it is a genuinely different architectural family from the
encoder--decoders that occupy the benchmark, so if the ceiling is a property of
the task rather than of one architecture, a detector should hit it too. Second,
it is the deployment-relevant one: handheld probes push echocardiography to the
bedside, where inference must run on an embedded accelerator alongside a live
image stream.

Applying a detector to CAMUS is not, however, a matter of changing the
dataloader. An instance mask is supervised as a \emph{single closed polygon},
and one of the three CAMUS structures --- the myocardium --- is an annulus. A
polygon has no hole. It is natural to assume that how one resolves this matters,
and we show that it does not, along with almost everything else one might tune.

\subsection{Contributions}

\textbf{An encoding for nested cardiac structures, and its ceiling.} We define
the second class as the \emph{filled} epicardial disc and recover the myocardium
by subtraction, keeping the mask head on simply-connected shapes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:encoding}). We quantify what such an encoding can express by
converting ground truth to polygons, rasterising it back and scoring it against
the untouched reference. At 96 vertices this reaches $0.997$ Dice and
$0.308$\,mm HD95 --- exactly one pixel at the CAMUS spacing. The polygon
representation is therefore not what limits a detector on this task.

\textbf{A measured noise floor, and ablations read against it.} Before
interpreting any comparison we train one configuration under three seeds. The
spread is $0.134$\,mm HD95, and a paired Wilcoxon test over the 200 test frames
calls two such runs significantly different ($p{=}0.035$). We therefore treat
$0.134$\,mm as the smallest credible effect. This is not a formality: several of
the differences we would otherwise have reported as significant, and several
reported in the literature, are smaller than this.

\textbf{Thirteen factors, no improvements.} We sweep encoding, vertex budget,
model scale and generation, input resolution, mask-prototype resolution,
augmentation recipe, pretraining, training-set size, a boundary-weighted loss,
the three loss gains and the optimiser --- roughly fifty training runs on one
laptop GPU (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). None improves on a stock configuration
beyond the noise floor. Higher-resolution mask prototypes are significantly
\emph{worse}. What does register is training-side: augmentation and
initialisation.

\textbf{A like-for-like comparison, including latency.} We evaluate on the
official CAMUS split, verified byte-identical to the released subgroup lists,
against nine semantic-segmentation baselines under one protocol, with HD95 at
native resolution in millimetres. Because published latencies are measured on
heterogeneous hardware, we re-time every model ourselves under a single
protocol, at two input resolutions, which shows the detector to be
near-invariant to resolution where encoder--decoders scale with pixel count.

\textbf{A fault in the reference EF implementation.} The official CAMUS
ejection-fraction code selects a contour by position rather than by area. This
is invisible on ground truth, which is single-component, but model predictions
carry occasional spurious blobs: on one baseline, $6\%$ of frames, which was
sufficient to drive EF correlation from $r{=}0.825$ to $r{=}0.080$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:ef}). Any EF computed from predictions with the reference code at
native resolution is unreliable without a connected-component filter.

Our aim is not a leaderboard position. It is to establish, with the variance
measured rather than assumed, which of the choices practitioners routinely tune
actually matter on a benchmark that has reached annotator agreement --- and to
document the measurement pitfalls we encountered, several of which affect
published numbers.
